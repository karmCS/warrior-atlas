Capoeira is played rather than fought, which makes it the strangest entry on any map of martial arts. Two people enter a ring of singers and clapping hands called the roda and move to whatever tempo the berimbau sets, and the instrument alone decides whether the exchange stays conversational or turns fast enough to hurt someone. The base step, the ginga, never stops, so a capoeirista is always in transition and never planted in a stance. Kicks come out of cartwheels, handstands, and low sweeps, and attacks are evaded rather than blocked, which is why so much of the art happens upside down.

It was made by enslaved and free Africans and their descendants in colonial Brazil, and the first time the record names it, in Rio court papers from 1789, it is describing a crime. An 1890 penal code banned it outright. Mestre Bimba opened an academy in Salvador in the 1930s and built Capoeira Regional, Mestre Pastinha followed in 1941 keeping the older Angola style intact, and UNESCO listed the art in 2014.
