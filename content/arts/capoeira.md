Capoeira developed among enslaved and free Africans and their descendants in colonial Brazil, most closely associated with Bahia, where its codified modern styles later took shape. Court records from Rio de Janeiro first name the practice "capoeiragem" in 1789, describing it as a criminal offense. The exact date of origin is unknown; Wikidata's own inception estimate of around 1600 is a rough approximation, not a documented founding year. Capoeira mestres and much of the oral tradition trace the art to Angola. Scholar T. J. Desch-Obi has argued for a specific ancestor, engolo, a kicking and evasion contest from the Cunene region of southern Angola. Other historians treat the engolo link as a later-adopted origin story, not settled genealogy, and capoeira's relationship to other proposed forerunners, batuque, and more tentatively moraingy from Madagascar and French savate, remains unresolved.

By the nineteenth century, street capoeira in Rio de Janeiro had become tied to gang violence, and an 1890 penal code banned it outright. The shift from outlawed street fighting to organized art came in the 1930s, when Manuel dos Reis Machado, known as Mestre Bimba, opened an academy in Salvador and created Capoeira Regional. Vicente Ferreira Pastinha followed in 1941, founding a school that preserved the older Angola style. Both began admitting women as students, breaking with earlier practice. Bira Almeida, known as Mestre Acordeon, helped bring capoeira to the United States in the 1970s, and UNESCO added it to its Intangible Cultural Heritage list in 2014.
