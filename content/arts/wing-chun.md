Wing Chun fights in a corridor. Everything is arranged around the centreline, an imaginary vertical line down the middle of the body, and the rule is to take it and keep it, striking straight down the middle on the shortest path available so the attacking arm is already sitting where a block would go. It works close, near enough that hands are felt rather than seen. Chi sao, sticking hands, drills exactly that, with two people joined at the forearms reading pressure and following it.

Who built it is unknown, and at least eight lineages tell the story differently. The version told most often has a Shaolin nun, Ng Mui, teaching a young woman named Yim Wing-chun, though she is treated as a mythical originator rather than a person with a record. The first well-documented practitioner is Leung Jan, a Foshan herbalist who began teaching privately around 1870; his student Chan Wah-shun later trained a boy named Ip Man, who moved to Hong Kong around 1949 and opened the art to the public there. Bruce Lee was one of Ip Man's Hong Kong students, and he eventually judged the art too rigid, building Jeet Kune Do out of that criticism. You might've seen Wing Chun's growth through the "Ip Man" films, Donnie Yen's biopic series starting in 2008 that turned Ip Man himself into the art's global face.
