Kenwa Mabuni began training under Ankō Itosu in Shuri around age thirteen, absorbing the direct, powerful lines of Shuri-te. His friend Chōjun Miyagi later introduced him to Kanryō Higaonna, and Mabuni took up Naha-te's tighter, more circular methods as well. Most Okinawan karateka of his generation stuck to one lineage or the other; Mabuni trained seriously in both, and that dual grounding became the point of everything he built afterward.

By 1929 he had moved to Osaka to teach karate full-time on the mainland, calling his approach Hanko-ryu, "half-hard style," a name meant to capture the mix of hard Shuri-te and softer Naha-te body mechanics. Around 1931 he settled on a permanent name, Shitō-ryū, built from the on'yomi readings of the first kanji in his two teachers' names: "Shi" from Itosu, "Tō" from Higaonna. The Dai Nippon Butoku Kai formally registered the style in 1939.

Because Shitō-ryū drew from two separate curricula rather than one, it kept an unusually large kata list; sources put the count anywhere from the fifties into the nineties depending on the branch, well beyond Shotokan or Gōjū-ryū. One of Mabuni's senior students, Chōjirō Tani, later split off his own line, Shūkōkai, in the late 1940s. Mabuni died in Tokyo in 1952; his son Kenei took over the school.
