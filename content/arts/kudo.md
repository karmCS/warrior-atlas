Kudo solved karate's face-punch problem with equipment. Fighters wear a clear moulded helmet, and behind it the rules open all the way up: full-contact strikes standing, judo throws, and ground work with a short clock before the referee stands everyone back up. It also does something almost no other combat sport does about size. Competitors are matched on an index that adds height in centimetres to weight in kilograms, so a tall light fighter and a short heavy one land in the same bracket, and reach is treated as an advantage worth counting rather than an accident of the weigh-in.

Takashi Azuma came up through judo, joined Mas Oyama's Kyokushin organization in 1971, and won the All Japan in 1977, where other fighters called him the human locomotive for his low kicks. He retired in 1979 with a complaint: facing larger opponents abroad had convinced him that Kyokushin's rules, no face punches and no throws, left a smaller man no way to close the gap. On 17 February 1981 he founded Daido Juku in Sendai, striking from Kyokushin and grappling from judo, and renamed the system Kudo in 2001.
