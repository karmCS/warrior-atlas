Sambo took shape in the Soviet Union in the early 1920s, when the Red Army wanted better hand-to-hand combat training for its soldiers. Two men built it along separate, sometimes rival lines. Vasili Oshchepkov studied judo in Japan under Kano Jigoro, earning a nidan black belt in 1917, and had already opened Russia's first judo school in Vladivostok in 1914. By the late 1920s he coached judo at the Red Army's sports club and the Moscow Physical Education Institute, blending it with Soviet folk wrestling. Viktor Spiridonov worked separately at the Dynamo sports society in Moscow, building a softer system shaped partly by a war injury to his arm. Oshchepkov was arrested during the Great Purge in 1937 on a fabricated spying charge and died in custody. He did not live to see the USSR All-Union Committee for Physical Culture and Sport recognize the discipline on November 16, 1938, credited largely to his student Anatoly Kharlampiev. The name "sambo," short for samozashchita bez oruzhiya (self-defense without weapons), only came into common use in the 1940s. Wikidata lists no coordinates for sambo; Moscow is used here since both founders worked there and the recognizing authority was based there. FILA accepted sambo as a third international wrestling style in 1968, and the IOC granted it provisional recognition in 2018. No martial art in the sources checked lists sambo as an ancestor. Sambo has visibly shaped modern MMA grappling, but no documented lineage ties it to Brazilian jiu-jitsu or any other named art.
