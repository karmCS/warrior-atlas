Sambo is jacket wrestling with the leg locks left in. The kurtka gives grips much like a judo jacket, but the shorts and wrestling boots underneath say where the rest of it came from. Heel hooks, kneebars, and ankle locks were legal from the start, at a point when judo had already ruled most of them out, and sport sambo bans chokes outright, which pushes a match further toward the legs than any comparable art. Combat sambo puts the strikes back in and is a different animal entirely.

Unlike most martial arts, it was commissioned. The Red Army wanted better hand-to-hand training in the early 1920s, and Vasili Oshchepkov, who had studied judo in Japan under Jigoro Kano, spent the decade blending judo with Soviet folk wrestling while Viktor Spiridonov worked on a softer system at Dynamo in Moscow. Oshchepkov did not live to see it recognized, arrested in the Great Purge in 1937 on a fabricated spying charge and dead in custody, and the credit in 1938 went largely to his student Anatoly Kharlampiev. The name abbreviates samozashchita bez oruzhiya, self-defence without weapons.
