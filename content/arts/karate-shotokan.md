Shotokan is the long-range end of karate. The stances are deep and the techniques are big, which parks a fighter further out than most styles are comfortable with and then crosses that gap in one committed movement. Nothing about it is subtle, and that is deliberate; the deep stances are a training load before they are a fighting position, building the legs at a cost in mobility the style accepts on purpose. It is also the version most beginners meet, because large techniques performed in unison are the easiest thing in karate to teach to a full room.

Gichin Funakoshi trained in Shuri under Ankō Itosu, and in 1922 the Ministry of Education invited him to Tokyo to demonstrate at Japan's first national athletic exhibition. It went well enough that Jigoro Kano asked for a second performance at the Kodokan, after which Funakoshi simply stayed. The style owes as much to his son Yoshitaka, who pushed the kata toward those long, low stances. Students built him a dojo in Mejiro in 1936 and hung a sign reading Shoto-kan, his pen name joined to hall, a name he did not choose himself.
