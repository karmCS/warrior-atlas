Gichin Funakoshi trained in Shuri, Okinawa, under Ankō Itosu and Ankō Asato, absorbing the Shuri-te tradition that later became known as Shorin-ryu. In 1922 the Japanese Ministry of Education invited him to Tokyo to demonstrate karate at the country's first national athletic exhibition. It went well enough that Jigoro Kano asked him to perform again at the Kodokan, and Funakoshi stayed on in Tokyo rather than return home, teaching first at a dormitory for Okinawan students and then at university clubs including Keio and Waseda.

The style that emerged was shaped as much by his son, Yoshitaka (Gigo) Funakoshi, who pushed the kata toward the longer, lower stances and more overtly athletic technique still associated with the style today. In 1936 Funakoshi's students built him a dedicated dojo in Mejiro and hung a sign over the entrance reading "Shoto-kan," combining his pen name Shoto, meaning pine waves, with kan, meaning hall. Funakoshi did not choose the name himself; his students gave it to him. The building was destroyed in the 1945 Tokyo air raids.

In 1949 senior students, among them Masatoshi Nakayama and Hidetaka Nishiyama, founded the Japan Karate Association in Tokyo to standardize instruction and grading, with Funakoshi serving as honorary head. Two of Funakoshi's own students later founded their own styles: Hironori Otsuka, who received one of Funakoshi's first black belts in 1924, developed Wado-ryu, and Masutatsu Oyama, who trained under both Funakoshis before turning to Goju-ryu, founded Kyokushin.
