Muay Thai, called the "art of eight limbs" for its use of fists, elbows, knees, and shins, grew out of older Siamese fighting traditions grouped under the name muay boran, alongside the armed style krabi krabong. Wikipedia's infobox dates documented practice in Thailand to the 18th century, though some accounts point to unarmed combat training in a 13th-century military manual attributed to King Ram Khamhaeng, a claim historians treat with caution given how few records survive from that period. By 1455, under King Borommatrailokkanat, the Ayutthaya court had built hand-to-hand training into the King's Guard Department. The often-repeated story of Nai Khanom Tom, a captured fighter said to have beaten ten Burmese opponents to win his freedom, shows up in Thai sources only starting in the 20th century; most scholars now read it as a nation-building legend rather than a recorded event.

The sport took its modern ring form in Bangkok. A permanent ring went up at Suan Kulap College in 1921, Suan Sanuk Stadium followed in 1923, and King Rama VII's government set down formal rules, weight classes, and gloved competition between 1925 and 1935. Rajadamnern Stadium (1945) and Lumpinee Stadium (1956) later became the sport's two main venues. In the 1950s, Japanese karateka Tatsuo Yamada combined Muay Thai with karate to create kickboxing, and the hybrid style Kudo, developed by Takashi Azuma's Daido Juku school, later folded in Muay Thai techniques of its own.
