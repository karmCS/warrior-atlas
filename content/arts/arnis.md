Known interchangeably as Arnis, Kali, or Eskrima, the national martial art of the Philippines is one of the very few systems that teaches the weapon before the empty hand. A beginner is handed a pair of rattan sticks in the first lesson, and the reason is that the stick itself is not the point. The angles are the point; a stick, a knife, a machete, or a rolled umbrella all travel the same lines, so one drill covers every one of them. Empty-hand technique comes last, taught as the same pattern with nothing in the hand.

Almost everything before the 1600s is oral history, and the traceable record starts in Cebu with Doce Pares in 1932, Balintawak in the 1950s under Venancio "Anciong" Bacon, and Remy Presas building Modern Arnis from 1966. A 2009 law made it the national art. Its wider fame came through Dan Inosanto, who brought the stick and blade work into Bruce Lee's Jeet Kune Do, and through Hollywood, where Kali flow drills are the reason fight choreography in films like The Bourne Ultimatum and John Wick looks the way it does.
