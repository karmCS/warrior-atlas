Boxing's origins split into two stories: an unarmed ancient sport, and a codified modern one from England. Reliefs from Sumer and paintings in Egyptian tombs show men fist-fighting as early as the third millennium BC. A few researchers push the practice back further, to roughly the sixth millennium BC in what is now Ethiopia, though evidence is thin and not widely accepted. Boxing entered the ancient Olympics in 688 BC, with Greek fighters wrapping their hands in leather thongs called himantes.

The version that shaped the modern sport took hold in England. Jack Broughton, an undefeated champion, wrote the first formal rules in 1743 after an opponent died in a bout; the rules gave a downed fighter thirty seconds to rise and banned hitting a man while down. The London Prize Ring Rules replaced Broughton's code in 1838, revised in 1853, keeping fights bare-knuckle while banning head-butting, eye-gouging, and biting. In 1867, John Graham Chambers drafted the Marquess of Queensberry Rules, requiring padded gloves, three-minute rounds, and a ten-count. Named for the Marquess who backed them, the rules ended bare-knuckle prizefighting and spread to the United States.

Wikidata records no parent martial art for boxing, and Wikipedia's infobox has no lineage field either; this entry treats boxing as originating from ancient fist-fighting rather than any single named predecessor. It later shaped other arts: Bruce Lee listed boxing among Jeet Kune Do's influences, mainly for its footwork, and 1970s American kickboxing promoters, including Joe Lewis, paired boxing's punches with karate's kicks.
