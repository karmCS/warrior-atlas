Boxing is the most restricted of the major striking arts, and every fighter who has borrowed from it has borrowed because of that restriction rather than in spite of it. Two closed fists, nothing below the waist, no clinching, no kicks, no throws. With no legs to check and no arms to trap, the defence has to be carried entirely by the feet and the head, which is why boxers slip, roll, and pivot to a degree no other art demands.

The sport people watch today is English. Jack Broughton wrote the first set of rules in 1743 after an opponent died in a bout, the London Prize Ring Rules replaced them in 1838 and kept fights bare-knuckle, and in 1867 John Graham Chambers drafted the Marquess of Queensberry Rules, requiring padded gloves, three-minute rounds, and a ten-count. Everything before that is ancient and disconnected, with fist-fighting in Sumerian reliefs and an Olympic event by 688 BC. Today its hands and footwork are cross-trained by strikers who have no interest in boxing itself.
