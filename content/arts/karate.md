Karate developed on Okinawa, then the independent Ryukyu Kingdom, from a native fighting tradition called te blended with Chinese martial arts brought back through trade and study trips to Fujian province, including elements of Fujian White Crane. By the 18th and 19th centuries the art took different shapes in different towns, known as Shuri-te, Naha-te, and Tomari-te. No single town is credited as karate's sole birthplace, so the coordinates here mark Naha, the Ryukyu capital and site of the Naha-te lineage, as a representative point rather than a confirmed founding location. The long-standing claim that karate arose because the Satsuma clan banned weapons after invading Okinawa in 1609 is disputed: researchers have found the 1613 order restricted carrying swords, not owning them, and was loosely enforced.

Matsumura Sōkon, Ankō Itosu, and Higaonna Kanryō, who trained in Fuzhou, shaped the art through the 1800s. Itosu added karate to Shuri's school physical education classes in 1901, and his student Gichin Funakoshi brought it to mainland Japan in the 1920s, borrowing judo's uniforms and colored belt ranks from Jigoro Kano along the way. Japanese masters changed the character for "kara" from one meaning "China" to a homophone meaning "empty" in 1933, made official at a 1936 roundtable in Naha, partly to distance the art from China as relations between the two countries worsened. Karate reached the United States after World War II through returning servicemen and debuted as an Olympic sport at the 2020 Tokyo Games.
