Chinese martial arts is a heading rather than an art. Hundreds of family styles sit under it with little in common beyond the country, and the oldest way of sorting them is geographic, with northern systems favouring long kicks, jumps, and wide stances while southern ones work short hand strikes from a low rooted base. What almost all of them share is the form. Taolu are long fixed sequences performed alone, and they carry a style's entire syllabus the way a written record would, though whether a form is a fighting method or a filing system is an argument as old as the styles themselves.

Both English names are loanwords: kung fu is Cantonese for any skill earned through practice, and wushu is Mandarin for martial art. The documented record is thin, with a 728 AD stele as the oldest evidence of Shaolin fighting and most named styles, tai chi among them, dating to the Qing or later. The Kuomintang surveyed the schools as guoshu from 1928, and the People's Republic reverted to wushu and standardized the forms from 1958. Competition wushu is now judged on difficulty and presentation rather than fighting, and practitioners still disagree over whether that preserved the arts or hollowed them out.
