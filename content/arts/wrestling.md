Wrestling has no founder and no single homeland. Cave art in France from roughly 15,000 to 17,000 years ago shows grappling, and wrestling appears in Egyptian and Mesopotamian art from around 2000 BCE. It entered the ancient Olympics in 708 BCE, and Plato, Aristotle, and Socrates all wrote about it. Since no country can claim the sport, this entry anchors on documented dominance rather than legend.

Georgia's chidaoba, an indigenous style with roughly 200 named holds, trained combatants into the late Middle Ages before becoming sport; UNESCO listed it as heritage in 2018. Wrestling is Georgia's most successful Olympic sport, with 21 medals since independence in 1994, striking for a country of under four million. The Soviet Union's state sports-school system made it wrestling's most decorated 20th-century power, with 116 Olympic medals and 62 golds, mostly Greco-Roman. That legacy is sharpest in Russia's Dagestan republic, population roughly 3.1 million, producing over twenty Olympic golds since 1992, three at the 2024 Paris Games alone. That density is why this entry's coordinates sit at Makhachkala, Dagestan's capital, not a national one. Iran's koshti pahlavani, tied to the zurkhaneh tradition, still supplies most of Iran's Olympic medals. Turkey's oiled yağlı güreş has run at Kırkpınar since 1362, reportedly the oldest continuously held sporting competition on record. Mongolia's bökh runs just as deep culturally but has produced far fewer Olympic medals. Sumo, sometimes folded into "wrestling," is catalogued separately on Wikidata with its own country of origin and no formal link to this sport.
