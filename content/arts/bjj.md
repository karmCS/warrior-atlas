Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu is built on the assumption that a fight ends up on the ground whether anyone planned it or not, and that once both people are down, size matters far less than angle. A smaller grappler on their back is not losing; the guard is a fighting position rather than a pinned one, which is the idea that separates BJJ from every other grappling art. Position comes before submission, so a practitioner passes the legs, takes side control, then the mount or the back, and only then attacks the arm or the neck.

It began as judo that travelled. Mitsuyo Maeda settled in Belém around 1914 and Carlos Gracie fell into his circle by 1917. Carlos' younger brother Hélio was too small for the standard throws, so he rebuilt the art around leverage, and the academy the two opened in Rio in 1925 is the usual start date. The Gracies were never the only line; Luiz França trained under Maeda as well and passed the art to Oswaldo Fadda, whose students later beat several Gracie fighters using the footlocks Hélio had dismissed. Royce Gracie's run at UFC 1 in 1993 took it global.
