Brazilian jiu-jitsu grew out of Kodokan judo, brought to Brazil by Mitsuyo Maeda, a judoka who settled in Belém, Pará, around 1914 after years touring the world in jiu-jitsu challenge matches. Carlos Gracie began training in Maeda's circle around 1917. The Gracie family has long said Maeda taught Carlos personally; historian Robert Drysdale argues Carlos learned mainly from Maeda's assistant, Jacyntho Ferro. Carlos's younger brother Hélio, too small for judo's standard throws, reworked the art around leverage and ground control. The family later moved to Rio de Janeiro, where Carlos and Hélio opened an academy in 1925, the date most cited as the sport's start; Wikidata records an earlier inception of 1910.

A separate, non-Gracie lineage grew from the same root. Luiz França, who also trained under Maeda in Belém, passed the art to his student Oswaldo Fadda, who taught for free in Rio's Bento Ribeiro district and built a reputation for footlocks, a technique Hélio reportedly dismissed as "suburban" before Fadda's students beat several Gracie fighters. Wikipedia's infobox lists jujutsu, judo, and catch wrestling as the lineage; Wikidata classifies the art as a subclass of judo and jujutsu but has no such link to wrestling, and its "derived from" field is empty for all three. The art's later role in shaping mixed martial arts, especially through Royce Gracie's run at UFC 1 in 1993, appears throughout its histories, but Wikidata lists no martial art formally derived from it besides car-jitsu, a novelty grappling variant too marginal to list here.
