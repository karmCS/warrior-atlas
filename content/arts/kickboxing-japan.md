Kickboxing's Japanese branch traces to karate instructor Tatsuo Yamada, who spent the 1950s testing full-contact rules because standard karate sparring barred direct strikes. Yamada, who trained under Chōki Motobu and ran his own school under the name Nihon Kempo Karate-do, floated the term "karate-boxing" for his idea in November 1959. A Muay Thai exhibition at Asakusa town hall in Tokyo on December 20, 1959, sharpened his thinking about a hybrid sport built for real contact. Boxing promoter Osamu Noguchi picked up the idea, studied Muay Thai, and fused it with karate into a new sport he named kickboxing. Noguchi founded the Kickboxing Association in 1966, and the first sanctioned bout was staged in Osaka on April 11 that year.

Television carried the sport through the 1970s, with kickboxing airing on three Japanese channels a week by 1970. The boom ended abruptly around 1980 as ratings fell and coverage thinned out. Kazuyoshi Ishii revived the format in 1993 under the K-1 banner, and the promotion grew into the largest kickboxing organization in the world.

An American branch developed independently starting around 1970, when karate fighter Joe Lewis staged the first full-contact bout in the United States. Both branches trace back to karate, but the Japanese and American scenes built their ring-sport rules on separate continents roughly a decade apart, with no direct hand-off between them. This entry covers the Japanese line; see [[kickboxing-usa]] for the American one.
